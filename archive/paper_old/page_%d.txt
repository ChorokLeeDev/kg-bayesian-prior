                                                         Abstract

 1             Among Energy scoring’s 100 most confident predictions on knowledge graphs,
 2             78%±3% have zero training evidence—the model has never seen these entity-
 3             relation pairs. This systematic overconfidence affects every major uncertainty
 4             method (variational embeddings, ensembles, energy scoring, foundation models)
 5             and 11–25% of test queries across seven benchmarks.
 6             We trace the failure to a structural mismatch: entity-level uncertainty cannot detect
 7             relation-specific novelty. High-frequency entities receive low uncertainty regardless
 8             of which relation is queried, causing methods to be most confident where they have
 9             least evidence. On temporal KGs with ground-truth timestamps (ICEWS14/18),
10             all baselines collapse to chance while a simple coverage check—tracking which
11             (entity, relation) pairs appeared in training—achieves 0.99 AUROC.
12             The fix is simple: track coverage via hash table. The contribution is quantifying and
13             explaining a pervasive failure mode—affecting 11–25% of queries—that aggregate
14             evaluation metrics have hidden.


15   1    Introduction
16   An Empirical Discovery. Among Energy scoring’s 100 most confident predictions on FB15k-237,
17   78%±3% have zero training evidence—the model has never seen these entity-relation pairs, yet
18   assigns them highest confidence. This is 2.4× higher than the 32% base rate (Table 2). The pattern
19   holds across methods: UKGE shows 80%±4%, and both methods exhibit 1.5–2.1× elevation even
20   after controlling for entity frequency. This is not miscalibration in the usual sense—it is systematic
21   overconfidence on zero-evidence queries.

22   The Failure is Pervasive. Across seven benchmarks, 11–25% of test queries involve novel re-
23   lational contexts: familiar entities appearing in relationships never observed during training. Ev-
24   ery major uncertainty method—variational embeddings [Chen et al., 2019, 2021], Bayesian ap-
25   proaches [Choudhary et al., 2021], ensembles, energy scoring, and even foundation models like
26   ULTRA [Galkin et al., 2024]—fails identically on these queries (AUROC ≤ 0.50). The failure has
27   remained hidden because standard evaluation aggregates over query types, masking category-specific
28   collapse.

29   Why This Happens. The explanation is intuitive: entity e appearing in 1000 triples with relations
30   {r1 , . . . , r10 } receives low variance (well-observed), yet queries (?, r11 , e) are genuinely uncertain.
31   Any uncertainty depending only on entity identity cannot distinguish these cases. We formalize this
32   in Theorem 2: under empirically-verified conditions, relation-agnostic uncertainty achieves AUROC

38   Contributions. (1) Empirical discovery: We identify systematic overconfidence on zero-evidence
39   queries—78% of top-confident predictions lack any training evidence. This finding, not previously
40   reported, reveals a fundamental evaluation gap. (2) Prevalence quantification: 11–25% of test
41   queries across seven benchmarks fall into this blind spot, making it a substantial rather than edge-case
42   failure. (3) Theoretical explanation: Theorem 2 formalizes why the failure occurs, providing testable

44   KGs with ground-truth timestamps (ICEWS14/18), coverage achieves 0.99 AUROC—confirming the

47   2   Related Work

48   Probabilistic KG Embeddings. UKGE [Chen et al., 2019], BEUrRE [Chen et al., 2021], and
49   GP-KGE [Choudhary et al., 2021] learn uncertainty over KG embeddings, but all produce relation-
50   agnostic variances. KG2E [He et al., 2015] assigns learned Gaussians per entity and relation type—
51   fixed parameters, not conditioned on whether a specific entity has been observed with that relation.
52   Despite training on entity-relation co-occurrence patterns, these methods do not learn relation-specific
53   uncertainty, necessitating explicit decomposition. Relation-aware scoring (R-GCN [Schlichtkrull
54   et al., 2018], CompGCN [Vashishth et al., 2020]) conditions predictions on relations but still produces
55   relation-agnostic uncertainty; our decomposition addresses uncertainty independently of scoring
56   architecture.

57   OOD Detection. MC Dropout [Gal and Ghahramani, 2016], Deep Ensembles [Lakshminarayanan
58   et al., 2017], Energy [Liu et al., 2020b], and SNGP [Liu et al., 2020a] capture model uncertainty
59   but not structural observation patterns. When applied using entity embeddings (as done here), they
60   remain relation-agnostic. Graph Posterior Networks [Stadler et al., 2021] and GNNSafe [Wu et al.,
61   2023] propagate uncertainty via graph structure but target node classification rather than triple-level
62   KG OOD. The OpenOOD benchmark [Zhang et al., 2023] provides stratified evaluation for images;
63   our stratified KG evaluation (novel-context vs emerging-entity) is analogous but addresses relational
64   structure.

65   Temporal KGs and Conformal Prediction. Temporal KG methods [García-Durán et al., 2018,
66   Lacroix et al., 2020] model when facts hold but do not address uncertainty quantification. Conformal
67   prediction approaches [Zhu et al., 2025a,b] provide prediction set coverage guarantees rather than


                                                                          2
 68   OOD detection; our decomposition complements them by identifying which uncertainty type drives
 69   unreliability.

 70   Foundation KGE. ULTRA [Galkin et al., 2024] enables zero-shot link prediction on unseen
 71   KGs. We hypothesize ULTRA inherits the coverage blind spot: its NBFNet architecture encodes
 72   connectivity but not which specific relations each entity has seen. Empirical validation confirms this:
 73   ULTRA achieves 0.29–0.31 AUROC on novel contexts, below random, indicating anti-predictive
 74   behavior.

 75   Positioning. Prior work treats uncertainty as a single quantity. We show KG uncertainty decomposes
 76   into semantic (entity-level) and structural (relation-specific) components, with validated predictions
77    on when each is necessary.

78    3     Background
 79   Knowledge Graphs. A knowledge graph G = (E, R, T ) contains entities E, relation types R, and
 80   triples T ⊆ E × R × E. Each triple (h, r, t) asserts that relation r holds between head entity h and
 81   tail entity t.

 82   OOD Detection Task. Given a trained model, distinguish in-distribution (ID) test triples from out-
 83   of-distribution (OOD) corruptions. Following Bordes et al. [2013], the standard protocol generates
 84   OOD triples by replacing the tail with a random entity: (h, r, t) → (h, r, t′ ) where t′ ∼ Uniform(E).
 85   However, this creates easily detectable corruptions (low model scores). We additionally evaluate on
 86   temporal OOD—emerging entities and novel relational contexts—which are harder to detect (§5).
 87   Performance is measured by AUROC.

 88   Variational KG Embedding Uncertainty (GP-KGE). Probabilistic embedding methods [Choud-
 89   hary et al., 2021] learn Gaussian distributions over entity embeddings:

 90   where µi , ℓi ∈ Rd are the mean and log-variance for entity i. Uncertainty for a triple (h, r, t) is

100   KGE” only when referencing the original method; our framework uses semantic uncertainty (Usem )
101   to denote the diagonal posterior variance of a learned entity embedding, avoiding GP terminology.

102   4     Method
103   4.1   Preliminaries

104   Frequency: freq(e) counts triples containing entity e. Coverage: c(e, r) ∈ {0, 1} indicates whether
105   e appears with r in training.
106   Definition 1 (OOD Partition). Fix threshold τ > 0. OOD queries partition into: Emerging
107   entities Demerge : min(freq(h), freq(t)) ≤ τ . Novel contexts Dnovel : min(freq(h), freq(t)) > τ and
108   (c(h, r) = 0 or c(t, r) = 0). We set τ to the 25th percentile of entity frequencies.


                                                         3
109   Novel-context detection by Ustr is perfect by construction (the same c(e, r) defines both the OOD
110   label and the detector). The contributions are: (1) proving all embedding-based methods are blind to
111   novel contexts (Theorem 2), (2) demonstrating this empirically—78% of top-confident predictions
112   have zero evidence (Table 2), and (3) quantifying that 11–25% of test queries fall into this blind spot.
113   See Remark 6.

114   4.2   Uncertainty Signals

115   Semantic uncertainty (entity embedding variance): Usem (h, r, t) = 12 (σ̄h2 + σ̄t2 ) where σ̄e2 is the
116   mean diagonal variance. This is relation-agnostic: it depends only on entity variances, not the query
117   relation.
118   Structural uncertainty (coverage): Ustr (h, r, t) = 2 − c(h, r) − c(t, r) ∈ {0, 1, 2}.

119   4.3   Theoretical Results

120   Theorem 2 (Limitation of Relation-Agnostic Detection). Let U (h, r, t) = f (σh2 , σt2 ) where σe2
121   depends only on entity e, and f is any combining function. Under assumptions A1–A3 (Appendix A.1):
                                                               1
                                          AUROC(U, Dnovel ) ≤ + O(ϵ)                                (3)
                                                               2
122   where ϵ measures frequency-matching tightness. Assumptions validated: Spearman ρs (freq, σ 2 ) ≤
123   −0.68 on all datasets; ≥98% of novel-context triples have frequency-matched ID counterparts.
124   Proof sketch. By A1, σe2 is determined by freq(e). By A3, novel-context triples have frequency-
125   matched ID counterparts, so U (h, r, t) ≈ U (h′ , r′ , t′ ), giving AUROC ≈ 1/2. Full proof in Ap-
126   pendix A.1.
127   Definition 3 (Relation-Agnostic Embedding). An embedding ϕ : E → Rd is relation-agnostic if
128   I(ϕ(e); c(e, r) | freq(e)) = 0 for all r.
129   Theorem 4 (Embedding-Based Limitation). Let ϕ satisfy Definition 3. For any U (h, r, t) =
130   F (ϕ(h), ψ(r), ϕ(t)): AUROC(U, Dnovel ) ≤ 1/2 + O(ϵ).
131   Key insight. Real embeddings violate Definition 3—trained DistMult achieves AUC=0.96 for
132   predicting coverage within frequency strata. Yet novel-context AUROC remains 0.34–0.50 (Table 1).
133   Why? Coverage information exists in embeddings but is architecturally inaccessible: standard
134   uncertainty methods (variance, energy, ensembles) cannot extract it without explicit supervision.
135   Coverage-aware training fails to generalize: AUROC on held-out unseen pairs decreases from 0.63
136   to 0.58 (Appendix A.3). This confirms explicit coverage tracking is necessary.

137   Empirical Regularity 1: Complementarity. Semantic and structural uncertainty exhibit comple-
138   mentary failure modes: (i) Semantic achieves AUROC ≈ 0.50 on novel contexts (Theorem 2). (ii)
139   Structural achieves AUROC < 1 on emerging entities (some have coverage). (iii) The combination
140   gain is dominated by emerging entities:
                            AUROC(Ucomb ) − AUROC(Ustr ) ≈ πe · Acomb             str
                                                                                         
                                                                       emerge − Aemerge                       (4)
141   This holds on all 7 datasets (Table 1); Eq. 4 matches observation within 0.002 (Appendix B.2).
142   Theorem 5 (Information-Theoretic Characterization). Let ρ = P (Ustr = 0|Ye = 1) be coverage
143   overlap. Then: (i) I(Ustr ; Yn ) = H(Yn ): structural captures all novel-context information. (ii)
144   I(Usem ; Yn |Ustr ) = 0: semantic adds nothing for novel contexts. (iii) I(Usem ; Ye |Ustr ) > 0 iff ρ > 0:
145   semantic helps on emerging entities only when coverage overlap exists.
146   On static benchmarks (ρ = 0.34–0.66), semantic provides +8–11pp gains; on ICEWS14/18 (ρ ≈ 0),
147   semantic adds nothing—both predicted exactly.

160   5     Experiments

161   5.1   Experimental Setup

162   Datasets. Static benchmarks with simulated temporal OOD: FB15k-237 (14.5K entities, 237
163   relations), WN18RR (40.9K, 11). Temporal KGs with ground-truth timestamps: ICEWS14 (7.1K,
164   230), ICEWS18 (23K, 256), where OOD categories emerge from chronological splits—breaking the
165   definitional coupling between coverage and OOD labels.

166   Baselines. UKGE [Chen et al., 2019], Energy [Liu et al., 2020b], MC Dropout [Gal and Ghahramani,
167   2016], Deep Ensembles [Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017], SNGP [Liu et al., 2020a], GNNSafe [Wu
168   et al., 2023], and single signals (Usem , Ustr ). All share identical splits, hyperparameters (dim=100,
169   lr= 10−3 , 30 epochs), and evaluation protocol.

170   Metrics. AUROC (primary). WN18RR/ICEWS18 use 5 seeds; FB15k-237/ICEWS14 use 10 seeds.
171   Statistical significance: CAGP vs. baselines p < 0.001; effect sizes d > 5 (Appendix C.12).

172   5.2   Main Results

173   Key findings (Table 1). Non-circular evidence (ICEWS14/18): On temporal KGs with ground-truth
174   timestamps, Ustr achieves 0.99 AUROC while baselines collapse to 0.34–0.87. This is not circular—
175   OOD is defined by timestamp, not coverage. Semantic gains on static benchmarks: Semantic variance
176   adds +8–11pp on emerging entities over coverage alone (WN18RR: +7.7pp, FB15k-237: +10.5pp;
177   both p < 0.001). Circularity disclosure: On static benchmarks, novel-context AUROC=1.0 by
178   construction; these are diagnostic, not performance claims.

179   5.3   Validating the 78% Confident-Wrong Finding

180   Table 2 reveals score-based methods are most confident on zero-evidence queries. Among Energy’s
181   top-100 predictions, 78% have zero training evidence (vs. 32% baseline). These are also incorrect:
182   87% have Error@1. This is not about coverage—it’s an empirical discovery about Energy’s failure
183   mode.
184   Table 3 confirms Empirical Regularity 1: Usem achieves 0.34–0.40 on novel contexts (significantly
185   below 0.5, p < 10−3 ), confirming anti-predictive behavior consistent with Theorem 2.

186   5.4   Adversarial Evaluation: ICEWS14 Strict Split

187   Inverse relations can inflate temporal OOD performance. We construct a strict split removing all
188   inverse-relation overlap (58.5% removed).
189   Energy collapses to chance (0.59→0.50, p < 0.001); CAGP maintains 1.00 AUROC (Table 4).

190   5.5   Negative Result: GDELT Role-Shift

191   CAGP fails when coverage overlap is high. On GDELT role-shift (ρ = 0.84, 500K entities): semantic
192   achieves 0.50 AUROC (random), structural achieves 0.58, CAGP achieves 0.57. When 84% of
193   OOD has full coverage, neither signal works. Role-shift OOD requires relation-conditioned methods
194   beyond our scope.

195   5.6   Cross-Domain: Biomedical KGs and Foundation Models

196   Hetionet. On drug-disease relations, 61.7% are novel contexts. Energy achieves 0.50 (random);
197   Coverage achieves 1.00. Under inductive splits (new diseases), 99% of predictions involve coverage
198   blind spots.

199   ULTRA. The KG foundation model achieves 0.29 AUROC on novel contexts—below random—
200   confirming Theorem 4: scale does not fix the blind spot.

201   5.7   Ablations

202   Removing Lmargin causes <0.1pp change; reparameterization is essential (without it: 0.92→0.72,
203   p < 0.001). Standard random-corruption OOD: CAGP ties best single signal (Appendix B.10).

204   6   Conclusion
205   We discover that KG uncertainty methods are systematically overconfident on zero-evidence queries:
206   78% of Energy’s top-100 predictions have no training evidence. This affects 11–25% of test queries
207   across seven benchmarks—a substantial failure mode hidden by aggregate evaluation.
208   The cause is structural: entity-level uncertainty cannot detect relation-specific novelty. High-frequency
209   entities receive low uncertainty regardless of which relation is queried. Theorem 2 formalizes this,
210   and our experiments confirm it holds across variational embeddings, ensembles, energy scoring, and
211   foundation models (ULTRA achieves 0.29 AUROC—below random).

212   Practical recommendations.
213         1. Track entity-relation coverage. A hash table catches the failure mode invisible to learned
214            methods. For billion-entity KGs, Bloom filters reduce storage 5× with <0.2pp recall loss.
215         2. Flag zero-coverage queries. When c(e, r) = 0, the model has no evidence for this
216            combination.
217         3. Report stratified metrics. Aggregate AUROC hides category-specific failures.

218   Limitations. Coverage tracking fails on role-shift OOD (GDELT: 0.57 AUROC) where entities
219   use atypical relations but have full coverage. This requires relation-conditioned methods beyond our
220   scope. The approach assumes transductive settings; inductive KGs need different mechanisms.

221   Reproducibility.    Code, models, and data splits released upon publication.

222   Ethics statement. This work presents a diagnostic analysis of existing methods and does not
223   introduce new risks beyond those inherent to knowledge graph systems. The primary practical
224   recommendation—tracking entity-relation coverage—improves system reliability by flagging predic-
225   tions with no training evidence. All datasets used (WN18RR, FB15k-237, YAGO3-10, ICEWS14,
226   ICEWS18, GDELT, Hetionet) are publicly available research benchmarks. We do not foresee negative
227   societal impacts from this work; if anything, identifying when models are unreliable should improve
228   trustworthiness of deployed KG systems.

321   A.2    Proof of Theorem 2 (Impossibility Result)

322   Theorem statement. Any uncertainty estimator U (h, r, t) = f (σh2 , σt2 ) where σe2 depends only on
323   entity e achieves AUROC ≤ 1/2 + O(ϵ) on novel contexts under assumptions A1–A3.
324   Proof. Consider the OOD detection task distinguishing DID from Dnovel . By definition, novel contexts
325   have min(freq(h), freq(t)) > τ (high frequency entities) but c(h, r) = 0 or c(t, r) = 0 (unobserved
326   entity-relation pairs).
327   Step 1: Variance depends only on frequency. By A1, the learned variance σe2 is a monotonically
328   decreasing function of freq(e). In practice, this emerges from Bayesian posterior updates: entities
329   with more observations have tighter (lower variance) posteriors. Therefore, σe2 = g(freq(e)) for some
330   decreasing function g.
331   Step 2: Novel contexts have frequency-matched ID counterparts. By A3, for any novel-context
332   triple (h, r, t) ∈ Dnovel , there exists an ID triple (h′ , r′ , t′ ) ∈ DID such that |freq(h) − freq(h′ )| ≤ ϵh
333   and |freq(t) − freq(t′ )| ≤ ϵt for small ϵh , ϵt .
334   Step 3: Uncertainty scores are indistinguishable.

343   A.3    Proof of Theorem 4 (Embedding-Based Impossibility)

344   Theorem statement. Let ϕ : E → Rd be a relation-agnostic embedding (Definition 3), and let ψ :
345   R → Rk be any relation embedding. For any uncertainty estimator U (h, r, t) = F (ϕ(h), ψ(r), ϕ(t))

347   Proof. By Definition 3, I(ϕ(e); c(e, r) | freq(e)) = 0, meaning ϕ(e) is conditionally independent of
348   coverage given frequency. Thus any function F (ϕ(h), ψ(r), ϕ(t)) can only use frequency information
349   to distinguish novel contexts from ID.
350   Step 1. For novel context (h, r, t) where c(h, r) = 0, the embedding ϕ(h) contains no information
351   about whether h has seen relation r beyond what freq(h) reveals.
352   Step 2. By A3, there exists ID triple (h′ , r′ , t′ ) with |freq(h) − freq(h′ )| ≤ ϵ. By Step 1, the
353   conditional distribution P (ϕ(h) | freq(h)) does not depend on c(h, r). Therefore, conditioned on
354   freq(h) ≈ freq(h′ ), the distributions P (ϕ(h)) and P (ϕ(h′ )) are statistically indistinguishable—not

356   Step 3. Therefore U (h, r, t) = F (ϕ(h), ψ(r), ϕ(t)) ≈ F (ϕ(h′ ), ψ(r′ ), ϕ(t′ )) = U (h′ , r′ , t′ ), giving
357   AUROC ≈ 1/2.

444   A.6     Extended Remarks on Theoretical Results

445   Remark 6 (Relationship to structural detector). The novel-context category is defined via the same
446   coverage indicator c(e, r) used by Ustr . This means structural uncertainty achieves perfect separation
447   on novel contexts tautologically by design—not through empirical discovery.
448   To be clear: the contribution is not that coverage detects zero-coverage (which is trivial). The
449   contributions are:

450         (a) The impossibility theorem: proving that all embedding-based methods—variational, ensem-
451             ble, energy, or otherwise—are fundamentally blind to novel contexts (Theorem 2).
452         (b) The 78% confident-wrong finding: demonstrating that existing methods do not merely fail
453             to detect zero-evidence queries—they assign highest confidence to them (Table 2). This is an
454             empirical discovery about Energy/UKGE behavior, not a tautology about coverage.
455         (c) Prevalence: novel contexts constitute 11–25% of realistic test sets (WN18RR: 10.8%,
456             FB15k-237: 25.4%, YAGO: 12.6%), making the blind spot practically critical.

457   The circularity objection conflates the trivial fix (coverage tracking) with the non-trivial diagnosis
458   (why all learned methods fail). We expose the problem; the solution’s simplicity is a feature, not a
459   limitation.
460   Remark 7 (Logical structure of Theorem 2). The theorem formalizes a precise logical structure:
461   given that variance is frequency-determined (A1) and novel-context entities are frequency-matched to
462   ID (A3), the inability to discriminate follows directly. The non-trivial empirical content is threefold:
463   A3 holds in practice (≥98% matching), the result rules out any f (σh2 , σt2 ) including sophisticated
464   learned combinations, and the anti-predictive direction (AUROC < 0.5) is non-obvious. The held-out
465   relation experiment (Appendix C.13) confirms structural necessity independently.
466   Remark 8 (Role of assumptions). Assumptions A1–A6 are idealized sufficient conditions. In practice,
467   A4 is violated on all benchmarks (Appendix A.5). Nevertheless, the qualitative predictions hold
468   across all datasets and seeds, suggesting the theorem captures the correct structural insight even
469   when formal conditions do not strictly hold.

470   Remark 9 (Structural detection of novel contexts). Ustr achieves AUROC = 1 on novel contexts by
471   construction: novel contexts have c(e, r) = 0 (Definition 1), giving Ustr ≥ 1, while ID has Ustr = 0
472   (A2). The substantive claims are the impossibility of entity-level detection (Theorem 2) and the
473   prevalence of novel contexts (11–25%).

474   A.7   Proof of Theorem 5 (Information-Theoretic Characterization)

475   Part (i): Structural captures all novel-context information. By definition, novel contexts have
476   c(h, r) = 0 or c(t, r) = 0, so Ustr ≥ 1. ID has Ustr = 0 by A2. Therefore Yn is a deterministic
477   function of Ustr (for high-frequency entities): Yn = 1[Ustr ≥ 1 ∧ min(freq(h), freq(t)) > τ ]. This
478   implies H(Yn |Ustr ) = 0, so I(Ustr ; Yn ) = H(Yn ) − H(Yn |Ustr ) = H(Yn ).

479   Part (ii): Semantic adds no information for novel contexts. Given Ustr ≥ 1, we already
480   know Yn = 1 (for high-frequency entities). Therefore H(Yn |Ustr , Usem ) = H(Yn |Ustr ) = 0.
481   By the data processing inequality, conditioning on additional variables cannot increase entropy:
482   I(Usem ; Yn |Ustr ) = H(Yn |Ustr ) − H(Yn |Ustr , Usem ) = 0.

483   Part (iii): Semantic helps on emerging iff ρ > 0. Let ρ = P (Ustr = 0|Ye = 1) be the fraction of
484   emerging entities with coverage for the query relation.
485   Case ρ = 0: All emerging entities have Ustr > 0, so they are perfectly separated from ID (which has
486   Ustr = 0). Therefore H(Ye |Ustr ) = 0, and I(Usem ; Ye |Ustr ) = 0.
487   Case ρ > 0: Some emerging entities have Ustr = 0 (same as ID). In this stratum, Usem provides
488   discrimination if emerging entities have systematically higher variance than ID entities (verified
489   empirically: emerging entities have lower frequency, hence higher variance by A1). The conditional
490   mutual information I(Usem ; Ye |Ustr = 0) > 0 when Usem reduces uncertainty about Ye within the
491   Ustr = 0 stratum.

492   Empirical validation. Table 5 reports ρ (A5) across datasets: FB15k-237 (0.43), WN18RR (0.34),
493   YAGO (0.66). All have ρ > 0, predicting positive semantic gains—confirmed in Table 3 (+8–12pp).
494   On ICEWS14/18, nearly all emerging entities have Ustr > 0 (68% of ICEWS14 emerging entities are
495   absent from training entirely), so ρ ≈ 0 and semantic provides negligible gain—exactly as observed.
496   □

498   B.1   Alpha Sensitivity Analysis

499   Why alpha cannot be learned from the link prediction objective. In principle, α could be
500   treated as a learnable parameter. However, the link prediction loss provides zero gradient with
501   respect to α: all training triples have Ustr = 0 by construction (both entity-relation pairs are observed
502   in training), so ∂LBCE /∂α = 0. The uncertainty margin loss Lmargin does involve OOD triples
503   (random corruptions during training), but random entity corruptions rarely produce zero-coverage
504   triples, providing insufficient signal to learn α. In preliminary experiments, the logit-parameterized α
505   remained at its initialization (0.50) across all datasets and seeds, confirming the flat loss landscape.

506   Post-hoc sensitivity. Since α only affects the uncertainty computation (not model training), we
507   evaluate sensitivity by training CAGP once per seed and sweeping α ∈ {0.0, 0.05, 0.1, . . . , 1.0} at
508   evaluation time. This is computationally free.

510   yields 0.923, and α=1 yields 0.626 (semantic only). On FB15k-237: 0.935, 0.967, and 0.418. On
511   YAGO3-10: 0.840, 0.899, and 0.440 (Figure 2). Performance is robust across α ∈ [0.05, 0.95],

535   Explanation. Binary coverage achieves perfect OOD detection because novel contexts are char-
536   acterized by zero coverage—entity-relation pairs never observed during training. Binary coverage
537   provides clean separation: ID triples have both entities observed with the query relation (uncertainty
538   = 0), while OOD triples have at least one entity never observed with that relation (uncertainty > 0).
539   Continuous coverage introduces frequency-based variations among observed pairs. An entity seen
540   once with a relation gets low frequency weight, while an entity seen 100 times gets high frequency
541   weight. This creates overlapping uncertainty distributions between ID (which can have low-frequency
542   observations) and OOD (which may include entities observed frequently with other relations). The
543   result is near-random performance (AUROC ≈ 0.56–0.59).
544   This validates that the discrete presence/absence signal is fundamental to structural uncertainty, not
545   the continuous frequency information.

546   Why perfect detection on novel contexts? Binary coverage achieves AUROC = 1.0 on novel
547   contexts by construction (Remark 6): the novel-context category is defined by c(e, r) = 0, and the

556   B.5   Selective Prediction

557   We present an oracle analysis of selective prediction (abstaining on high-uncertainty queries) on
558   FB15k-237 link prediction (seed 42, 2000-triple subset), designed to illustrate the upper bound
559   on error reduction achievable by each uncertainty signal. Related work includes confidence-based
560   abstention for temporal KG reasoning [Hou et al., 2024]. The setup follows the standard selective
561   prediction framework [Geifman and El-Yaniv, 2017]: given a link prediction model that ranks
562   candidate entities, we use each uncertainty method to rank test triples by predicted uncertainty and


                                                        16
563   abstain from the top k% (highest uncertainty). “Accuracy” is Hits@1 on the remaining (100−k)%
564   of queries, computed using the base DistMult model’s entity rankings. We report results at 85%
565   coverage (k=15).

566   Oracle caveat. The abstention threshold is selected on the test set; this is not a deployable protocol
567   but an oracle evaluation that quantifies the potential of each uncertainty signal for selective prediction.
568   In a deployment setting, a held-out calibration set or conformal prediction framework [Zhu et al.,
569   2025b] would be required to set the threshold while guaranteeing a target coverage rate.

570   Methodology details. The 2,000-triple subset is drawn uniformly at random from the FB15k-237
571   test set (seed 42, fixed). For each test triple (h, r, ?), the trained DistMult base model ranks all
572   candidate tail entities by score s(h, r, t′ ); the model is not retrained for this analysis. The “correct”
573   prediction is Hits@1: whether the ground-truth t is ranked first among all |E| candidates. An “error”
574   is defined as Hits@1 = 0. Each uncertainty method produces U (h, r, tgold ) for the ground-truth
575   triple; we abstain from the 15% of triples with highest U and report Hits@1 on the remaining
576   85%. Error reduction is (errorsfull − errorsabstained )/errorsfull . All methods share the same base model
577   predictions—only the abstention ranking differs.

618   effective, why don’t learned embeddings discover it automatically? The impossibility theorem
619   (Theorem 2) provides the answer: standard link prediction objectives incentivize predictive accuracy,
620   not relation-specific uncertainty differentiation. The model learns entity-level statistics (frequency,
621   centrality) but has no signal to distinguish whether an entity’s uncertainty should depend on the query
622   relation. Explicit coverage tracking provides this missing inductive bias.

659   C      Implementation Details

660   C.1    OOD Split Methodology

661   Simulated temporal OOD. We create simulated temporal OOD splits by partitioning test sets based
662   on entity frequency and entity-relation coverage, capturing the key temporal challenge: detecting
663   when familiar entities appear in unfamiliar relational contexts.


                                                         20
664   Categorization protocol.      For each test triple (h, r, t):

665          • Emerging entities: min(freq(h), freq(t)) ≤ τ where τ is the 25th percentile of training
666            entity frequencies. These are rare or newly-appeared entities with few training observations.
667          • Novel contexts: min(freq(h), freq(t)) > τ and (c(h, r) = 0 or c(t, r) = 0), where c(e, r)
668            indicates whether entity e was observed with relation r during training. These are well-
669            established entities appearing in previously unobserved relational patterns.
670          • In-distribution: Both entities are frequent (> τ ) and both have coverage for the query
671            relation.

672   Rationale. This protocol captures temporal dynamics: in real temporal KGs, entity frequency
673   correlates with “age” (older entities have more observations), and novel contexts arise when estab-
674   lished entities form new relationships over time. The frequency threshold τ at the 25th percentile
675   ensures the “emerging” category captures genuinely rare entities while “novel contexts” includes
676   only well-observed entities.

677   FB15k-237 split statistics.    Our temporal OOD split yields:

678          • Emerging entities: 2,223 triples (10.9%, mean freq=3.2, median=2)
679          • Novel contexts: 5,193 triples (25.4%, mean freq=35.1, median=28)
680          • In-distribution: 13,050 triples (63.8%, mean freq=44.7, median=37)

681   The clear frequency separation (emerging: median 2 vs novel contexts: median 28) confirms that
682   these categories represent distinct OOD phenomena.

683   ICEWS14: ground-truth temporal validation. ICEWS14 [García-Durán et al., 2018] is a tempo-
684   ral KG containing 7,128 entities, 230 relations, and events from 2014 with ground-truth timestamps.
685   The train/test split is chronological (earlier timestamps for training, later for test), so temporal OOD
686   arises naturally from the evolution of geopolitical events. We apply the same categorization protocol
687   to test triples using entity frequencies and coverage computed from the training set. The resulting
688   split—1,264 emerging, 2,982 novel contexts, 8,976 ID—reflects genuine temporal dynamics: 32%
689   of test triples involve unseen entity-relation patterns (emerging or novel contexts). Crucially, the
690   novel-context category is now defined by time (these are entity-relation pairs that first appear in later
691   timestamps), not by coverage—breaking the definitional coupling (Remark 6) that affects the three
692   static benchmarks.

693   C.2   Training Configuration

694   Embedding dimension d = 100, learning rate 10−3 , averaged over 5–10 seeds (10 seeds for FB15k-
695   237 and ICEWS14; 5 seeds for WN18RR and ICEWS18; 3 seeds for YAGO3-10). WN18RR,
696   FB15k-237, and ICEWS14: batch size 1024, KL weight βKL = 0.001, 30 epochs. YAGO3-10: same
697   canonical pipeline (batch size 1024, βKL = 0.001, 30 epochs). Additional training details: uncertainty
698   margin loss weight wunc = 0.1, margin m = 0.3, gradient clipping (norm 1.0), 1 negative sample per
699   positive (uniform random tail replacement). We report mean±std over seeds; with n=3–10 seeds,
700   bootstrap p-values are not reliable for small n (minimum achievable p≈0.125 for n=3) and are not
701   reported.

702   Reparameterization sampling scope. Reparameterization sampling (Contribution 4) is ap-
703   plied to all datasets (WN18RR, FB15k-237, YAGO3-10, ICEWS14, ICEWS18) via the canon-
704   ical experiment script (run_wn18rr_temporal.py), which uses the main CAGP model in
705   src/models/coverage_augmented_gpkge.py. This produces the results reported in Table 1 and
706   Appendix C.11. The ICEWS14 component ablation (Appendix C.5) uses a separate self-contained
707   ablation script with a no-reparam CAGP variant; because entity variances do not differentiate without
708   reparameterization, the ablation’s CAGP (full) achieves Emerging=0.976 vs. the canonical CAGP’s
709   1.000 (Table 1). The ablation’s primary purpose is demonstrating structural necessity (alpha=1
710   collapses to 0.50), not matching the canonical model’s absolute performance.

711   Scalability Analysis. Memory complexity. CAGP’s coverage matrix C ∈ {0, 1}|E|×|R| requires:

712          • FB15k-237: 14,541 × 237 = 3.4M entries ≈ 13MB (dense float32)

854   C.13   Held-Out Relation Experiment: Non-Circular Static Validation

855   Remark 6 discloses a definitional coupling on the three static benchmarks: novel-context OOD
856   is defined by zero coverage, so a coverage-based detector achieves AUROC=1.0 by construction.
857   ICEWS14/18 break this coupling via ground-truth timestamps. Here we provide an additional
858   non-circular validation on static benchmarks by separating the OOD definition from the coverage
859   signal.

860   Protocol. We randomly hold out 20% of relation types before building the coverage matrix. The
861   model is trained on all training triples (to learn good embeddings), but the coverage matrix is
862   computed from only the remaining 80% of relation types. OOD test triples are defined as those whose
863   relation belongs to the held-out set; ID triples are those whose relation is in the training-relation set
864   and both entity-relation pairs are covered. Crucially, the OOD label is determined by the relation
865   holdout, not by coverage—the two signals are independent.


      Table 24: Held-out relation experiment on FB15k-237 and YAGO3-10 (3 seeds each). OOD labels
      assigned by held-out relation set (independent of coverage); coverage still achieves AUROC=1.0 by
      the same mechanism as in Remark 6, confirming structural necessity under an independently defined
      OOD criterion.

                 FB15k-237      42, 123, 456     3295–5129        0.51 ± 0.12         1.000 ± 0.000
                 YAGO3-10       42, 123, 456     485–1720         0.63 ± 0.17         1.000 ± 0.000

867   Interpretation. The primary evidential value of this experiment is what it shows about GPOnly:
868   achieving only 0.51 on FB15k-237, confirming Theorem 2—entities in held-out-relation triples
869   are individually well-observed, so their variances are frequency-matched to ID entities, leaving the
870   semantic signal near-random. On YAGO3-10, GPOnly achieves 0.63 ± 0.17 across 3 seeds (range


                                                          27
871   0.44–0.77), consistent with YAGO’s denser entity-relation structure providing partial separability;
872   the high variance (±0.17) reflects the same split-driven heterogeneity seen in FB15k-237. Coverage
873   achieves AUROC=1.0 mechanically: any test triple with a held-out relation has zero coverage in the
874   restricted coverage matrix by construction. The independence claim is therefore about the OOD label
875   (determined by the random relation holdout), not about the coverage detector (which achieves perfect
876   separation by the same structural logic).
877   The nOOD range of 3,295–5,129 across FB15k-237 seeds reflects heterogeneity in relation frequency
878   (some held-out relations are much more frequent than others depending on the random split); the
879   resulting GPOnly variance (±0.12) reflects this split-driven heterogeneity rather than pure model
880   variance.
881   Together, these results establish that structural necessity—coverage catching what semantic uncer-
882   tainty misses—holds even when the OOD definition is independent of the coverage signal, and that
883   GPOnly’s near-random performance on novel relational contexts is robust across different holdout
884   configurations.
