% Introduction - Single author, direct style

Knowledge graphs power critical applications: drug interaction prediction, fraud detection, recommendation systems.
When these systems make predictions about unseen entities or novel relationships, knowing \emph{when to trust the output} is essential for safe deployment.
This motivates out-of-distribution (OOD) detection---distinguishing reliable predictions from uncertain ones.

Probabilistic knowledge graph embedding methods address this by learning distributions over entity embeddings rather than point estimates.
GP-KGE~\citep{Chen2022} learns a variance $\sigma^2_e$ for each entity, capturing how ``uncertain'' the model is about that entity's representation.
The intuition is appealing: entities seen frequently should have low variance (well-constrained embeddings), while rare entities should have high variance.

\textbf{The limitation I identify.}
This intuition breaks down because the learned variance is \emph{relation-agnostic}.
Consider an entity $e$ that appears frequently with relation $r_1$ (e.g., \texttt{born\_in}) but never with relation $r_2$ (e.g., \texttt{works\_at}).
GP-KGE assigns a single variance $\sigma^2_e$ to entity $e$, which is the same for both relations.
But the model's uncertainty about $(e, r_1, ?)$ and $(e, r_2, ?)$ should differ---$e$ has been observed with $r_1$ but not with $r_2$.

This observation leads to the central insight of this paper:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Uncertainty in knowledge graphs decomposes into two orthogonal components: semantic uncertainty (embedding quality) and structural uncertainty (observation patterns). Neither alone is sufficient for OOD detection.}
\end{quote}

\textbf{Semantic uncertainty} captures how well-constrained an entity's embedding is, based on how frequently and consistently it appears in training.
GP variance captures this signal: rare entities have high variance, frequent entities have low variance.

\textbf{Structural uncertainty} captures whether a specific entity-relation combination has been observed.
I formalize this through \emph{coverage}: $c(e, r) = 1$ if entity $e$ appears with relation $r$ in training, else 0.
Coverage is relation-specific by construction, addressing exactly the limitation of GP variance.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item I identify a fundamental limitation in probabilistic KGE: learned variances are relation-agnostic and miss structural uncertainty.
    \item I propose a semantic-structural decomposition framework that separates these orthogonal uncertainty types.
    \item I derive a closed-form AUROC bound for coverage-based detection, validated empirically with $<$4\% error.
    \item I demonstrate that combining both signals (CAGP) achieves 0.87--0.96 AUROC with 14--32\% synergy over single components across three benchmarks.
\end{enumerate}

The key finding is not the CAGP algorithm itself---which is a simple linear combination---but the \emph{insight} that probabilistic KGE methods have a structural blind spot that cannot be learned away.
