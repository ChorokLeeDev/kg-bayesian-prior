The central insight of this work is that OOD detection in knowledge graphs requires distinguishing \emph{what kind} of distribution shift has occurred---a distinction invisible to relation-agnostic uncertainty estimators.
By decomposing uncertainty into semantic and structural components, we predict complementary failure modes: semantic variance captures emerging entities while structural coverage captures novel relational contexts, each compensating where the other fails.
The decomposition's predictions hold across all five evaluated datasets. Ground-truth temporal benchmarks (ICEWS14, ICEWS18) provide the strongest non-circular validation; the three static benchmarks confirm the per-category structural predictions (within 0.005 of theory) rather than independent detection. The improvement is largest on emerging entities (+8--12pp), confirming that the semantic signal provides genuine complementary value when properly trained.
The decomposition is architecture-agnostic (DistMult, TransE, ComplEx; Appendix~\ref{app:architectures}) and computationally lightweight (coverage is precomputed once).
These properties make it applicable to domains where KG reliability is critical, such as drug interaction prediction, clinical decision support, and recommender systems.

\paragraph{Practical implications.}
Our analysis yields three actionable recommendations: (1)~\emph{Always track entity-relation coverage} alongside embedding uncertainty---this is a zero-cost addition (a hash table lookup) that catches the failure mode invisible to learned variances. (2)~\emph{Use reparameterization sampling} in the scoring function when training variational KG embeddings; without it, KL regularization collapses variances, making semantic uncertainty meaningless. (3)~\emph{Report per-category OOD metrics} (emerging vs.\ novel contexts), not just overall AUROC---overall numbers obscure complementary failure modes (Table~\ref{tab:complementarity}).

\paragraph{Scalability considerations.}
CAGP's coverage matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{E}| \times |\mathcal{R}|}$ requires $O(|\mathcal{E}| \times |\mathcal{R}|)$ memory; sparse storage (hash tables) reduces this to $O(|\mathcal{T}|)$ with $O(1)$ inference, making it practical for large KGs (see Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}).

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The static-benchmark circularity noted above (Remark~\ref{rem:circularity}) means static results are diagnostic rather than fully independent; the ICEWS datasets address this. We tested bilinear scoring as a step toward relation-aware architectures (Appendix~\ref{app:bilinear}); full message-passing models (R-GCN, CompGCN) remain untested.
Coverage is binary; the approach assumes transductive settings; assumption A4 is violated on all three benchmarks ($\Delta \geq 1.0$), so formal guarantees should be interpreted qualitatively.
An oracle selective prediction analysis (test-set threshold; Appendix~\ref{app:selective}) shows up to 43\% oracle error reduction at 85\% coverage; validating this with held-out threshold selection remains future work.

Future directions include GNN encoders with relation-aware variance (R-GCN, CompGCN), adaptive per-relation mixing ($\alpha_r$), inductive settings, and application to retrieval-augmented generation where $U_{\text{str}}$ and $U_{\text{sem}}$ support differentiated abstention strategies.
