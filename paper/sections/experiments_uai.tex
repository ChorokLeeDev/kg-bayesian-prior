\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Datasets.}
Two static benchmarks with \emph{simulated temporal OOD} splits: \textbf{FB15k-237} (14.5K entities, 237 relations) and \textbf{WN18RR} (40.9K, 11). Three temporal KGs with ground-truth timestamps: \textbf{ICEWS14} (7.1K, 230), \textbf{ICEWS18} (23K, 256), and \textbf{GDELT} (500K entities, 20 relations), where OOD categories emerge from chronological splits---breaking the definitional coupling between coverage and OOD labels.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
UKGE~\citep{chen2019embedding}, Energy~\citep{liu2020energy}, MC Dropout~\citep{gal2016dropout}, Deep Ensembles~\citep{lakshminarayanan2017simple}, SNGP~\citep{liu2020simple}, GNNSafe~\citep{wu2023energy}, and single signals ($U_{\text{sem}}$, $U_{\text{str}}$). GNNSafe uses energy-based OOD scoring on a 2-layer MLP with the same hyperparameters as other baselines; we include it to test whether GNN-style architectures escape the relation-agnostic limitation. All share identical splits, hyperparameters (dim$=$100, lr$=10^{-3}$, 30 epochs), and evaluation protocol. Standalone $U_{\text{sem}}$ (GPOnly) reports raw entity variance; the CAGP-internal semantic component is rescaled (\S\ref{sec:method}).

\paragraph{Metrics and reproducibility.}
AUROC (primary; AUPR in Appendix~\ref{app:aupr}). WN18RR and ICEWS18 use 5 seeds; FB15k-237 and ICEWS14 use 10 seeds (Appendix~\ref{app:per_seed}). Per-seed breakdowns show standard deviations $\leq 0.005$ across all datasets; the coverage signal is deterministic (zero seed variance), and learned variances contribute $<$0.01 AUROC variation. \emph{Statistical significance}: On FB15k-237 (10 seeds), CAGP vs.\ $U_{\text{str}}$ yields a paired t-test $p < 0.001$ (mean diff = 0.032, 95\% CI: [0.029, 0.035]); on ICEWS14, the difference is not significant ($p = 0.18$) due to the ceiling effect. All CAGP/coverage vs.\ baseline comparisons (Energy, UKGE, MC Dropout, Deep Ensembles, SNGP) achieve $p < 0.001$ via paired t-test on FB15k-237 (10 seeds), with effect sizes $d > 5$. We report 95\% bootstrap CIs (10K resamples over seeds, not triples) for key comparisons in Appendix~\ref{app:significance}. Models with learned variance receive $\mathcal{L}_{\text{margin}}$ ($w_{\text{unc}}{=}0.1$); ablation in \S\ref{sec:ablations} confirms that $\mathcal{L}_{\text{margin}}$ contributes $<$0.1pp. We use DistMult as the base scorer for simplicity; Appendix~\ref{app:architectures} confirms OOD detection is architecture-insensitive (0.959--0.963 AUROC across DistMult, TransE, ComplEx), consistent with our theoretical prediction that detection depends on coverage structure, not scoring function. Base DistMult achieves MRR/Hits@10 of 0.21/0.34 (FB15k-237) and 0.27/0.42 (WN18RR), comparable to reported DistMult baselines~\citep{trouillon2016complex}. We include Mahalanobis distance~\citep{lee2018simple} as a geometric baseline: it achieves 0.685 AUROC on novel-context detection (vs.\ Coverage 0.94)---better than random (0.5) but far below explicit coverage tracking, confirming that relation-agnostic geometric methods cannot match structural signals.\footnote{Mahalanobis computes $(e - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (e - \mu)$ using class-conditional statistics; for KGs we use per-relation Gaussians fit on training entity embeddings.} We do not compare conformal prediction (targets prediction-set guarantees, not detection) or evidential methods (targets classification, not KG triple scoring).

\textbf{Key findings} (Table~\ref{tab:temporal_ood}).

\emph{Non-circular evidence (ICEWS14/18).} On temporal KGs with ground-truth timestamps, coverage dominates: $U_{\text{str}}$ achieves 0.99 AUROC on both ICEWS benchmarks, and CAGP matches or exceeds this. On ICEWS14 (10 seeds), CAGP reaches 0.99 overall AUROC with 1.00 on emerging entities---a +2pp emerging gain over $U_{\text{str}}$ alone (not significant, $p{=}0.18$, due to ceiling). \textbf{ICEWS18 is a solved case}: coverage alone achieves 0.99 AUROC across all OOD categories, leaving no room for semantic improvement. This is not a limitation but a \emph{confirmation}---Empirical Regularity~1 predicts zero semantic gain when $\rho \approx 0$ (68\% of emerging entities absent from training), and we observe exactly that. The gap between baselines (0.34--0.87) and coverage (0.99) is the core finding ($p < 0.001$ for all baseline comparisons, paired t-test).

\emph{Diagnostic evidence (static benchmarks).} On simulated OOD splits, semantic variance adds +8--11pp on emerging entities over coverage alone (WN18RR: 0.91 vs 0.83; FB15k-237: 0.89 vs 0.78; both $p < 0.001$, paired t-test). The mixture-AUROC decomposition (Eq.~\ref{eq:mixture_auroc}) serves as a \emph{consistency check}: predicted and observed AUROC match within 0.005 (Appendix~\ref{app:mixture}), confirming gains arise from the predicted mechanism (emerging-entity separation) rather than artifacts.

\emph{Circularity disclosure.} On static benchmarks, novel-context AUROC$=$1.0 is \emph{definitionally guaranteed}---the OOD label and coverage-based detector share the same indicator (Remark~\ref{rem:circularity}). We report these as consistency checks, not performance claims. The substantive claims are: (1) the limitation theorem (verified: baselines achieve 0.34--0.50 on novel contexts), (2) novel-context prevalence (11--25\%), and (3) semantic gains on emerging entities (+8--11pp). ICEWS14/18 provide non-circular external validation.

\emph{RelCondVar.} Outperforms CAGP on FB15k-237 (+1pp overall, +4pp emerging) but reduces to $U_{\text{str}}$ on sparse-relation KGs (WN18RR), where insufficient relation diversity prevents learning meaningful conditioned variance (\S\ref{sec:relcondvar}).

\emph{Why does semantic help on static but not temporal?} On ICEWS14/18, nearly all emerging entities have zero coverage for the test relation (68\% of ICEWS14 emerging entities are entirely absent from training; Appendix~\ref{app:leakage}), so $U_{\text{str}}$ already captures them---leaving no room for semantic tiebreaking. On static benchmarks (WN18RR, FB15k-237), 34--66\% of emerging entities \emph{do} have coverage for the query relation ($\rho$ in Table~\ref{tab:assumptions}), creating ties that semantic uncertainty breaks. Empirical Regularity~1 predicts exactly this: the gain is $\pi_e \cdot (A_{\text{emerge}}^{\text{comb}} - A_{\text{emerge}}^{\text{str}})$, which is positive only when emerging entities have non-trivial coverage overlap ($\rho > 0$).

\subsection{Validating Complementarity}

\paragraph{Systematic overconfidence on zero-evidence queries.}
Table~\ref{tab:confident_wrong} reveals that score-based methods are not merely failing to detect novel contexts---they are \emph{most confident} on them. Among Energy's top-100 most confident predictions on FB15k-237, 83\% have zero training evidence (vs.\ 32\% baseline). The elevation decays (83\%$\to$58\%$\to$53\%) but stays above 50\% up to K=1000 (5\% of test set). Notably, a \emph{U-shape} emerges: both extremes have elevated zero-evidence rates (top 20\%: 37\%, bottom 20\%: 40\%), while the middle falls below baseline (27\%)---Energy systematically pushes zero-evidence queries to both confidence tails. This is not a frequency confound: Energy selects \emph{low}-frequency entities (0.09$\times$ average). The anti-correlation between confidence and evidence is the core failure mode Theorem~\ref{thm:impossibility} characterizes.

\textbf{Why this finding is not circular.} A skeptical reader might object: ``Coverage detects zero-coverage---that's tautological.'' But Table~\ref{tab:confident_wrong} is \emph{not} about coverage. It shows that Energy (a learned, score-based method) assigns highest confidence to queries with zero evidence. This is an empirical discovery about Energy's failure mode, not a property of coverage. The contribution is diagnosing \emph{why} existing methods fail, not proposing coverage as a solution (which is trivial). The 83\% statistic demonstrates that deployed systems are systematically overconfident on exactly the queries they should flag as uncertain.

\subsection{When Coverage Alone Fails: Ambiguous Coverage Benchmark}
\label{sec:ambiguous_coverage}

To demonstrate that semantic uncertainty is \emph{necessary} (not merely complementary) when coverage overlap $\rho > 0$, we construct a synthetic benchmark where coverage alone cannot distinguish OOD:

\paragraph{Design.} 1000 entities split into ``common'' (30\%, high frequency) and ``rare'' (70\%, low frequency). Common entities appear with most relations in training; rare entities appear with a small subset. Test OOD consists of rare entities paired with relations they \emph{are covered for}---so $U_{\text{str}} = 0$, yet they remain OOD (low semantic confidence).

\paragraph{Results (3 seeds).} On the \texttt{emerging\_covered} subset (rare entities with coverage):

\midrule

$U_{\text{sem}}$ & \textbf{1.00} \\

Semantic provides \textbf{+31pp} over structural on this synthetic subset---an upper bound demonstrating that semantic uncertainty \emph{can be necessary} when $\rho > 0$. \subsection{Ablation Studies}
\label{sec:ablations}

\paragraph{Margin loss.}
Removing $\mathcal{L}_{\text{margin}}$ ($w_{\text{unc}}{=}0$) causes $<$0.1pp change on WN18RR and FB15k-237 (seed 42; Appendix~\ref{app:margin_ablation}), confirming CAGP's gains derive from the coverage-semantic decomposition, not the auxiliary training objective.

\paragraph{Baseline + coverage.}
Post-hoc coverage augmentation ($0.5 \cdot U_{\text{baseline}} + 0.5 \cdot U_{\text{str}}$) does not match CAGP: on WN18RR, Energy+Cov$=$0.53, MC~Dropout+Cov$=$0.81, CoverageOnly$=$0.86, vs.\ CAGP$=$0.92. On FB15k-237, MC~Dropout+Cov$=$0.92 vs.\ CAGP$=$0.97. The gap arises because baseline variances lack meaningful frequency-differentiation without reparameterization sampling (cf.\ variance collapse ablation below): their uncertainty contributes noise rather than signal within coverage strata. CAGP's reparameterization-trained variances produce a rank-preserving tiebreaker that separates emerging entities \emph{within} the $U_{\text{str}}{=}0$ stratum (where 34--66\% of emerging entities fall; Table~\ref{tab:assumptions}).

\paragraph{Variance collapse.}
Omitting reparameterization sampling collapses entity variances (AUROC: 0.92$\to$0.72 on WN18RR, $p < 0.001$). Without reparameterization, the KL term drives all variances toward the prior, destroying the semantic signal. The reparameterization trick is essential.

\paragraph{Threats to validity.}
Static novel-context AUROC$=$1.0 by construction; these are diagnostic, not performance claims. ICEWS14 strict split breaks this coupling. ICEWS18 is a solved case where coverage alone suffices ($\rho \approx 0$); semantic gains appear only when $\rho > 0$ (static benchmarks: +8--11pp).

\paragraph{Refuse-to-answer baseline.}
A natural alternative to uncertainty scoring is to simply refuse predictions when coverage$=$0 (novel context). This achieves \emph{perfect precision} on novel contexts (no incorrect answers because no answers). However, it sacrifices recall: 11--25\% of test queries go unanswered (Table~\ref{tab:assumptions}). In practice, KG systems must provide \emph{some} response with calibrated uncertainty, not silent refusal. Coverage tracking enables exactly this: flag novel-context predictions as high-uncertainty rather than refuse, allowing downstream systems to decide their own precision-recall tradeoff.

\paragraph{Standard OOD.}
On standard random-corruption OOD (Appendix~\ref{app:standard_ood_full}), CAGP ties the best single signal (0.82 on FB15k-237) and is marginally below $U_{\text{sem}}$ on WN18RR (0.66 vs 0.67): coverage provides no information when corruptions sample entities that already have the query relation in training. CAGP is designed for temporal OOD; standard OOD is a complementary setting where entity-level methods are sufficient.

\subsection{Limitation: GDELT Role-Shift (CAGP Fails)}
\label{sec:gdelt}

\textbf{CAGP provides minimal benefit when coverage overlap is high.} We present GDELT role-shift as a \emph{negative result} that exposes the limits of our decomposition. ICEWS14/18 have low coverage overlap ($\rho \approx 0$), making them ``solved cases'' where coverage alone suffices. GDELT tests what happens when coverage \emph{cannot} separate OOD from ID.

\textbf{Role-shift OOD.} We construct a role-shift split on GDELT (500K entities, 20 relations): entities appearing with relations \emph{atypical} for their historical profile (outside top 80\% by frequency). This yields 87,684 OOD triples with $\rho = 0.836$---the highest coverage overlap among all benchmarks. 84\% of OOD triples have full coverage for the query relation.

\textbf{Results (5 seeds, 30 epochs).} Table~\ref{tab:gdelt} summarizes the results. \textbf{All methods fail}: semantic achieves \textbf{0.50 AUROC} (random), structural achieves 0.58, and CAGP achieves 0.57---no improvement over structural alone. This is a clear failure case for our approach.

\textbf{Why does CAGP fail here?} Two factors combine to defeat our decomposition:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Coverage cannot help.} With $\rho = 0.84$, 84\% of role-shift OOD triples have the entity-relation pair already observed in training. Structural uncertainty $U_{\text{str}} = 0$ for most OOD queries, so coverage provides almost no discriminative signal (0.58 AUROC).
    \item \emph{Semantic cannot help either.} Role-shift entities are \emph{high-frequency}---they appear often in training, just with different relations. Entity-level variance $\sigma^2(e)$ reflects overall training frequency (Theorem~\ref{thm:impossibility}), not relation-specific evidence. High-frequency entities receive low variance regardless of which relation is queried, yielding random-chance detection (0.50 AUROC).
\end{enumerate}
The combination of two uninformative signals remains uninformative: CAGP achieves 0.57 AUROC, essentially matching coverage alone.

\textbf{Theoretical interpretation.} Empirical Regularity~1 predicts AUROC gain only when: (a) coverage has discriminative power ($\rho < 1$), OR (b) semantic has discriminative power (emerging entities have higher variance than ID). On GDELT role-shift, \emph{neither condition holds}. This is not a failure of the theory---it is exactly what the theory predicts. Role-shift OOD fundamentally requires \emph{relation-conditioned} uncertainty: detecting that an entity is using an atypical relation requires knowing which relations are typical \emph{for that specific entity}. Neither entity-level variance nor entity-relation coverage can capture this. RelCondVar (\S\ref{sec:relcondvar_exp}) addresses this limitation but requires auxiliary supervision.

\textbf{When to expect CAGP to fail.} Our decomposition works when OOD status correlates with (1) missing entity-relation coverage, or (2) low entity frequency. Role-shift OOD violates both: entities are well-covered \emph{and} high-frequency. Any deployment facing role-shift OOD should not rely on CAGP or coverage tracking alone.

\subsection{Escaping the Limitation: Relation-Conditioned Variance}
\label{sec:relcondvar_exp}

Theorem~\ref{thm:impossibility} applies to \emph{entity-level} uncertainty $\sigma^2(e)$ that ignores the query relation. A natural question: can we bypass the limitation by conditioning on relation?

\textbf{RelCondVar.} We learn $\sigma^2(e, r) = \text{MLP}([\mathbf{e}; \mathbf{r}])$ where $[\cdot;\cdot]$ denotes concatenation of entity and relation embeddings. For entity-relation pairs rarely seen in training, the variance remains high; for frequent pairs, it decreases. This is trained with an auxiliary objective encouraging variance to correlate with inverse $(e, r)$ co-occurrence frequency.

\textbf{Role-Shift OOD.} We construct a non-circular benchmark on ICEWS14 where entities use relations \emph{atypical} for their historical profile (outside their top 80\% by frequency). All 2,026 OOD triples have full coverage ($\rho = 1.0$), so structural uncertainty $U_{\text{str}} = 0$ for all---providing no signal.

\textbf{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:relcondvar} shows RelCondVar achieves \textbf{0.753 $\pm$ 0.017 AUROC} (5 seeds) while structural collapses to chance (0.500). The +25pp gap is significant ($p < 0.001$, paired t-test) and demonstrates that relation-conditioned variance \emph{can} provide signal when coverage fails.

\textbf{Caveat: Auxiliary supervision required.} An ablation removing the auxiliary objective ($w_{\text{aux}}=0$) yields AUROC of 0.501$\pm$0.112---indistinguishable from chance. We tested five alternative objectives to escape this circularity: (A) binary coverage supervision (seen/unseen, not frequency), (B) contrastive loss (seen vs.\ unseen relations), (C) reconstruction (predict relation from entity+variance), (D) KL regularization, and (E) extended training (100 epochs). Results: A=0.787, B=0.704, C=0.357, D=0.506, E=0.583. While A and B appear promising, their success stems from implicit frequency leakage: negative sampling over-represents frequent pairs among ``seen'' examples, and rare relations are disproportionately ``unseen.'' Role-shift OOD is \emph{defined} by frequency (atypical = low-frequency relations), so any successful detector must encode frequency information---making non-circular detection fundamentally impossible for this OOD type. This validates our practical recommendation: track coverage explicitly rather than attempting to learn it.

\subsection{Cross-Domain Validation: Biomedical KGs}
\label{sec:biomedical}

To test whether the coverage blind spot generalizes beyond standard benchmarks, we evaluate on \textbf{Hetionet}~\citep{himmelstein2017systematic}---a biomedical knowledge graph for drug repurposing (45K entities, 24 relation types, 2.25M edges).

\paragraph{Random split hides the problem.}
Under random 80/10/10 splits, Hetionet shows only 1.7\% novel-context rate---seemingly contradicting our 11--25\% finding on standard benchmarks. However, this aggregated metric masks \emph{relation-specific} blind spots:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Relation Type & Novel Context & Description \\
\midrule
DdG & \textbf{61.7\%} & Disease-downregulates-Gene \\
DuG & \textbf{58.6\%} & Disease-upregulates-Gene \\
DaG & \textbf{30.4\%} & Disease-associates-Gene \\
CbG & 8.5\% & Compound-binds-Gene \\
Overall & 1.7\% & Dominated by hub genes \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The low aggregate rate is driven by \emph{hub genes}---highly connected entities that appear with most relation types. But the \textbf{drug discovery-critical relations} (Disease-Gene) show 30--62\% novel-context rates, higher than any standard benchmark.

\paragraph{OOD detection validation.}
We train DistMult on Hetionet and evaluate OOD detection on novel-context queries (3 seeds):

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Method & Novel Context AUROC & Drug-Disease AUROC \\
\midrule
Energy & 0.503 $\pm$ 0.003 & \textbf{0.431} $\pm$ 0.042 \\
Coverage & \textbf{1.000} $\pm$ 0.000 & \textbf{1.000} $\pm$ 0.000 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Energy achieves \textbf{exactly random} (0.503) overall and \textbf{anti-predictive} (0.431) on drug indication relations---meaning it assigns \emph{higher confidence} to zero-evidence queries. This result is on \emph{structural} (inductive) splits, not random splits. The anti-predictive behavior occurs because Energy correlates with entity connectivity: well-connected drugs (many known interactions) receive high confidence even for untested diseases, while drug-disease pairs are inherently sparse ($\rho \approx 0$). Coverage achieves perfect detection.

\paragraph{Inductive split reveals 99\% OOD.}
To simulate realistic drug repurposing (predicting for new diseases), we construct an inductive split: 80\% of diseases for training, 20\% held out.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Category & Count & Percentage \\
\midrule
Novel context & 5,983 & 64.9\% \\
Emerging entity & 3,138 & 34.0\% \\
In-distribution & 101 & 1.1\% \\
\textbf{Total OOD} & \textbf{9,121} & \textbf{98.9\%} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{99\% of drug repurposing predictions for new diseases involve coverage blind spots.} This has direct safety implications: models will confidently predict drug-disease-gene pathways with zero training evidence.

\paragraph{Split strategy is decisive.}
OGBL-BioKG~\citep{hu2020open} provides a striking comparison: despite nearly identical coverage density to WN18RR (1.83 vs 1.84 relations per entity), BioKG shows only \textbf{0.4\% novel-context} vs WN18RR's \textbf{39\%}. The difference is entirely due to split strategy:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Dataset & Rel/Entity & Split Strategy & Novel Context \\
\midrule
BioKG & 1.83 & Random edge & 0.4\% \\
WN18RR & 1.84 & Structural & 39.2\% \\
FB15k-237 & 9.56 & Structural & 31.4\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Random edge splits (OGB default) preserve coverage overlap: if entity $e$ appears with relation $r$ in training, test edges are uniformly sampled from the same $(e,r)$ pairs. Structural splits (FB15k-237, WN18RR) deliberately include entity-relation combinations absent from training.

\paragraph{Implication.}
(1) Random splits systematically underestimate real-world OOD prevalence---explaining why some biomedical KG papers report high accuracy without acknowledging coverage blind spots. (2) Our coverage tracking recommendation is especially critical for biomedical applications where overconfident predictions can mislead therapeutic development. (3) Practitioners should prefer structural or temporal splits when evaluating OOD robustness.

\subsection{Foundation Models Inherit the Blind Spot: ULTRA Validation}
\label{sec:ultra}

A natural question: do foundation models trained on multiple KGs escape the limitation? We validate on \textbf{ULTRA}~\citep{galkin2024ultra}---the state-of-the-art KG foundation model using NBFNet~\citep{zhu2021neural} with relation-aware message passing.

\paragraph{Hypothesis.} ULTRA maps entities to representations via 6-layer Bellman-Ford message passing, but these representations are not conditioned on the query relation. Per Theorem~\ref{thm:embedding_impossibility}, ULTRA should achieve near-random AUROC on novel contexts.

\paragraph{Results (FB15k-237).}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Method & Novel Ctx & Emerging & Overall \\
\midrule
ULTRA & \textbf{0.29} & 0.46 & 0.37 \\
Coverage & 0.94 & 0.86 & 0.97 \\
Random & 0.50 & 0.50 & 0.50 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent\textit{Methodological note.} ULTRA is a pretrained foundation model evaluated from a single public checkpoint (ultra\_3g.pth); inference is deterministic (no dropout or sampling). We do not report variance because: (1) there is no training variance (the checkpoint is fixed), and (2) evaluation is deterministic given our fixed test split. The 0.29 AUROC reflects ULTRA's behavior on this specific split, not a statistical estimate. This single-run evaluation is appropriate: our claim is that ULTRA \emph{inherits the blind spot} (AUROC$<$0.5), not that it performs worse than alternatives by a statistically significant margin. The anti-predictive AUROC (below random) is robust: we verified that shuffling test split orderings does not change the result.

\textbf{ULTRA achieves 0.29 AUROC on novel contexts---below random.} This means ULTRA assigns \emph{high confidence} to queries with zero training evidence. High-connectivity entities produce confident predictions regardless of which relation is queried. The anti-predictive AUROC (below 0.5) arises because NBFNet learns path patterns from training data, but for novel $(e, r)$ pairs it has never observed paths involving that combination---yet still predicts confidently based on overall entity connectivity rather than relation-specific evidence.

\paragraph{Why this happens.} ULTRA's NBFNet learns rich entity representations from overall graph structure (multi-hop paths, connectivity patterns). But this does not encode \emph{which relations each entity has been observed with}. When queried with an unseen relation, ULTRA produces confident predictions because the entity embedding is well-constrained---exactly the failure mode Theorem~\ref{thm:embedding_impossibility} characterizes.

\paragraph{Implication.} Scale does not fix the coverage blind spot. ULTRA is trained on 3--50 KGs with up to 157 relation types, yet still cannot detect novel contexts. Any deployment using ULTRA (or similar foundation models) \textbf{must} implement explicit coverage tracking to flag zero-evidence queries.
