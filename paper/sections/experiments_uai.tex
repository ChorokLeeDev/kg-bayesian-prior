\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Datasets.}
We evaluate on three benchmarks: \textbf{FB15k-237} (14,541 entities, 237 relations), \textbf{WN18RR} (40,943 entities, 11 relations), and \textbf{YAGO3-10} (123,161 entities, 37 relations). We create \emph{simulated temporal OOD} splits by partitioning test sets based on entity frequency and entity-relation co-occurrence patterns, simulating the key temporal challenge: detecting when familiar entities appear in unfamiliar relational contexts (methodology detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:ood_splits}). We refer to these as ``temporal OOD'' throughout for brevity.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
Score-based (UKGE~\citep{chen2019embedding}, Energy~\citep{liu2020energy}), ensemble (MC Dropout~\citep{gal2016dropout}, Deep Ensemble~\citep{lakshminarayanan2017simple}), distance-aware (SNGP~\citep{liu2020simple}), and single signals ($U_{\text{sem}}$, $U_{\text{str}}$). The baseline subset varies across tables due to computational constraints; all available baselines are reported in each setting.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
AUROC (primary; AUPR in Appendix~\ref{app:aupr}). Statistical significance via paired bootstrap ($p < 0.01$).

\textbf{Theorem predictions vs.\ observations.} Table~\ref{tab:complementarity} checks each part of Theorem~\ref{thm:complementarity} on two datasets:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Part (i):} Semantic uncertainty achieves 0.42/0.40 on novel contexts (near-random, as predicted $\approx 0.5$)
\item \textbf{Part (ii):} Structural uncertainty achieves 0.78/0.93 on emerging entities (imperfect $< 1$, as predicted)
\item \textbf{Part (iii):} Structural AUROC $= 1.000$ on novel contexts across both datasets---expected by construction (Remark~\ref{rem:circularity}), but novel contexts constitute 21--25\% of test triples, making this signal practically important
\item \textbf{Part (iv):} CAGP achieves 0.99--1.00 overall, exceeding both single signals (0.54 semantic, 0.94--0.96 structural)
\end{itemize}

The complementarity pattern is consistent across datasets but with different signal strengths: on FB15k-237, semantic uncertainty is strong on emerging entities (0.83)---emerging entities have low frequency, hence large learned variance by Assumption A1, making them readily separable from ID---while structural is weaker (0.78); on WN18RR (only 11 relations), structural is already strong on emerging entities (0.93) but semantic is weaker (0.65). Even simple averaging yields strong results (0.89--1.00), showing the decomposition framework itself is effective; the learned $\alpha$ adds value primarily through improved normalization across OOD types (Table~\ref{tab:temporal_ood}: $+$0.04 on FB15k-237, $+$0.05 on YAGO) rather than per-type detection. YAGO3-10 shows the most balanced pattern (Table~\ref{tab:temporal_ood}: semantic 0.82, structural 0.76), suggesting neither signal dominates---consistent with CAGP's largest absolute gain on this dataset.

Table~\ref{tab:standard} shows standard OOD with random corruptions. Score-based methods excel (0.99) because random corruptions produce implausible triples with low prediction scores---a signal CAGP does not use. SNGP's distance-aware uncertainty (0.60--0.63) underperforms score-based methods because its GP output layer operates on entity-level features without conditioning on the specific relation, making it relation-agnostic like the methods analyzed in Theorem~\ref{thm:impossibility}; additionally, KG embedding spaces are not Euclidean, so distance-based uncertainty estimates lose discriminative power compared to direct score-based approaches. CAGP's 3\% gap (0.96 vs 0.99 on FB15k-237) reflects that random corruptions often substitute entities that share coverage with the original (structurally plausible but semantically wrong). CAGP remains competitive (0.87--0.96) while providing robust temporal performance that score-based methods lack entirely (0.44--0.64). In practice, the two approaches are complementary: a practitioner can threshold on prediction scores for corruption detection and on CAGP uncertainty for temporal drift, with no additional training cost.

\textbf{Different failure modes, different solutions.} Score-based methods (UKGE, Energy) detect implausible triples via low prediction confidence---this works for random corruptions ("Paris → president → Eiffel Tower") but fails for temporal shift where plausible new facts emerge. Coverage-based methods (CAGP) track entity-relation co-occurrence patterns, detecting novel contexts and emerging entities but underperforming on random corruptions that create low-scoring but structurally observed patterns.

\textbf{Practical recommendation.} Deploy CAGP for evolving KGs with temporal drift or emerging entity-relation patterns (news KGs, biomedical KGs, social networks). Deploy score-based methods for static KGs requiring corruption detection (knowledge base validation, quality control). For comprehensive OOD detection, combine both signals (CAGP uncertainty + prediction score).

\paragraph{Relation-conditioned variance ablation.}
We also evaluated a variant (RelCondVar) that replaces entity-level variance with a learned $\sigma^2(e,r) = \text{MLP}([e; r])$ while retaining coverage in the combination ($\alpha{=}0.5$). On temporal OOD, RelCondVar matches CAGP across all datasets (e.g., WN18RR: both 1.00; FB15k-237: both 0.99; see supplementary material). This confirms that the coverage signal---not the specific variance parameterization---drives temporal OOD detection. CAGP's simpler design (explicit binary coverage) is preferable for interpretability and computational efficiency.

\paragraph{Empirical vs theoretical AUROC.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:complementarity} predicts semantic AUROC $= 0.5$ on novel contexts, but Table~\ref{tab:complementarity} shows 0.40--0.42. This discrepancy arises because Assumption A3 is approximate: novel-context entities tend toward higher frequencies than the ID average, causing slightly \emph{lower} semantic uncertainty than ID. The prediction remains directionally correct (near-random).

\paragraph{Binary vs continuous coverage.}
To test whether co-occurrence frequency improves upon binary presence/absence, we evaluated continuous coverage formulations (log-scaled, TF-IDF) on temporal OOD. Binary coverage achieves AUROC $= 1.0$ on FB15k-237 novel contexts (expected by construction; see Remark~\ref{rem:circularity}), while continuous variants (log-scaled, TF-IDF) achieve only 0.56--0.59 (near-random). The key insight is that continuous coverage introduces frequency-based variations among observed pairs that obscure the binary observed/unobserved boundary, causing ID and OOD uncertainty distributions to overlap (Table~\ref{tab:coverage_ablation}). For emerging entities, GP variance already captures frequency through learned embeddings, making explicit frequency counts redundant.

\paragraph{Error analysis.}
\emph{Temporal OOD.} On WN18RR temporal OOD (AUROC $= 1.00$), CAGP achieves perfect separation because the combination of GP variance and coverage is sufficient: emerging entities have high variance, and novel contexts have zero coverage. The residual difficulty lies in emerging entities where coverage is non-zero ($\rho = 0.13$ on WN18RR; Table~\ref{tab:assumptions}), but GP variance compensates. On YAGO3-10 (AUROC $= 0.94$), the 6\% gap arises from emerging entities with partial coverage ($\rho = 0.68$) \emph{and} moderate GP variance, where neither signal alone is decisive.

\emph{Standard OOD.} On FB15k-237 (random tail corruption, 20K ID + 20K OOD), CAGP achieves 0.971 AUROC.
False positives (8.7\%) occur on triples with low-degree tails (mean degree 183 vs 583 for correctly classified ID).
False negatives (8.7\%) involve corrupted tails that coincidentally create higher-degree entities (mean 183 vs 37 for typical corruptions).
See Appendix~\ref{app:error_analysis} for detailed breakdown.

\paragraph{Threats to validity.}
Our temporal OOD splits are simulated from entity frequency and coverage rather than ground-truth timestamps.
The novel-context category is defined using the same coverage indicator as our structural detector, so structural AUROC $= 1$ on this subset is guaranteed by construction (Remark~\ref{rem:circularity}).
The non-trivial empirical finding is that (a) this category constitutes 25--28\% of test triples across datasets, and (b) all existing probabilistic methods fail on it (AUROC 0.42--0.64), confirming Theorem~\ref{thm:impossibility}.
Evaluation on temporal KGs with ground-truth timestamps (e.g., ICEWS14, GDELT) would further strengthen generalizability.
Our relation-conditioned variance ablation (RelCondVar) matches CAGP performance, suggesting the decomposition framework rather than variance parameterization is the key contribution; however, comparison with architecturally relation-aware models (e.g., R-GCN) remains future work.

\paragraph{Non-definitional evidence.}
While structural AUROC $= 1$ on novel contexts is by construction (Remark~\ref{rem:circularity}), three lines of evidence demonstrate value beyond this definitional link:
(a)~On \emph{emerging entities}---where coverage coupling is weakest ($\rho = 0.13$--$0.68$; Table~\ref{tab:assumptions})---the decomposition achieves 0.95--1.00 AUROC (Table~\ref{tab:complementarity}), exceeding the structural signal alone (0.78--0.93).
(b)~On \emph{standard OOD} (random corruptions, Table~\ref{tab:standard}), which uses no coverage-based labels, the decomposition achieves 0.87--0.96.
(c)~The impossibility theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:impossibility}) is independent of how OOD is defined: it shows entity-level uncertainty cannot detect \emph{any} category correlated with relational novelty.
