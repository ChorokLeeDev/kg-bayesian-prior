\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Datasets.}
We evaluate on four benchmarks spanning different structural properties:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{FB15k-237}: 14,541 entities, 237 relations (dense, diverse relations)
    \item \textbf{WN18RR}: 40,943 entities, 11 relations (sparse relations, hierarchical)
    \item \textbf{YAGO3-10}: 123,161 entities, 37 relations (large scale)
    \item \textbf{ICEWS14}: 7,128 entities, 230 relations, with daily timestamps (ground-truth temporal splits)
\end{itemize}

For ICEWS14, we train on January--September 2014 and evaluate OOD detection on October--December 2014, providing genuine temporal distribution shift rather than synthetic simulation.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Score-based}: UKGE confidence~\citep{chen2019embedding}, Energy-based~\citep{liu2020energy}
    \item \textbf{Ensemble}: MC Dropout~\citep{gal2016dropout}, Deep Ensemble (5 models)~\citep{lakshminarayanan2017simple}
    \item \textbf{Distance-aware}: SNGP~\citep{liu2020simple}---spectral-normalized GP that estimates uncertainty via distance to training data in feature space
    \item \textbf{Single signals}: Semantic-only ($U_{\text{sem}}$), Structural-only ($U_{\text{str}}$)
    \item \textbf{Ours}: CAGP, RelCondVar
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Metrics.}
AUROC (primary), AUPR, and F1 at threshold 0.5. Statistical significance via paired bootstrap (10,000 iterations, $p < 0.01$).

\paragraph{Measurement methodology.}

\textbf{(A1)}: We compute Spearman rank correlation between entity frequency and learned variance $\bar{\sigma}^2_e$ across all entities. Strong negative correlations ($-0.74$ to $-0.85$) confirm that high-frequency entities have lower variance.

\textbf{(A2)}: By construction of standard KG benchmarks, test triples use entity-relation pairs from training. We verify 100\% of ID test triples have both entities covered for the query relation.

\textbf{(A3)}: We compute KL divergence between the frequency distribution of entities in novel-context OOD versus ID. Values vary significantly: WN18RR shows good overlap (0.44 nats), while YAGO3-10 shows less overlap (2.54 nats) due to its larger entity space.

\textbf{(A4)}: We compute $\Delta$ using the 95th percentile of ID minus the 5th percentile of novel-context (robust to outliers). FB15k-237 satisfies $\Delta < 1$, while WN18RR and YAGO3-10 marginally violate it. Importantly, the theorem predictions (Parts i and iii) still hold empirically on all datasets, suggesting the bound is conservative.

\textbf{(A5)}: We measure the fraction $\rho$ of emerging-entity queries where both entities are covered. The observed AUROC consistently exceeds the predicted $1-\rho$, indicating the combination works better than the worst-case analysis suggests.

\textbf{(A6)}: Semantic AUROC on emerging entities ranges $0.73$--$0.91$. WN18RR shows weaker separation (0.73), consistent with its sparse relation structure making frequency-based uncertainty less discriminative.

\paragraph{Theory-practice gap.}
The theorem bounds are conservative: even when (A4) is marginally violated, the method achieves strong empirical performance. Two observations explain this:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Parts (i) and (iii) hold exactly}: Semantic AUROC on novel contexts is 0.47--0.48 ($\approx 0.5$), and structural AUROC on novel contexts is 1.00, matching theorem predictions.
    \item \textbf{Observed $>$ predicted for (A5)}: Structural AUROC on emerging entities (0.66--0.93) exceeds the naive bound $1-\rho$ (0.32--0.87), suggesting the proof's worst-case analysis is loose.
\end{itemize}
The combination achieves 0.96--0.97 AUROC despite assumption violations, validating that the decomposition framework is robust beyond its formal guarantees.

The value lies in (1) identifying that existing methods miss structural uncertainty, (2) proving the signals are complementary, and (3) demonstrating the failure mode on realistic temporal benchmarks.

\paragraph{Expected Calibration Error (ECE).}
We compute ECE by binning predictions into 10 equal-width bins based on predicted uncertainty, then measuring the gap between predicted and actual OOD rates:
\begin{equation}
    \text{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{10} \frac{|B_b|}{N} \left| \text{acc}(B_b) - \text{conf}(B_b) \right|
\end{equation}
where $B_b$ is the set of samples in bin $b$, $\text{acc}(B_b)$ is the fraction of correct predictions (ID samples), and $\text{conf}(B_b)$ is the mean predicted confidence ($1 - \text{uncertainty}$).

\paragraph{Key findings} (Table~\ref{tab:calibration}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Score-based methods are poorly calibrated} (ECE $>$ 0.28): High scores don't reliably indicate correctness under temporal shift.
    \item \textbf{SNGP slightly improves over ensembles} (ECE 0.183 vs 0.198): Distance-awareness helps marginally, but remains relation-agnostic.
    \item \textbf{Single signals show moderate calibration} (ECE 0.14--0.16): Each captures partial uncertainty structure.
    \item \textbf{CAGP achieves 37\% ECE reduction} over the best single signal (0.089 vs 0.142).
    \item \textbf{RelCondVar achieves best calibration} (ECE 0.073): Learning relation-conditioned variance produces well-calibrated uncertainties.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Why decomposition improves calibration.}
Score-based methods conflate prediction confidence with OOD detection---a high-scoring triple may still be OOD if it involves novel relational contexts. Our decomposition separates these concerns: structural uncertainty captures observation patterns while semantic uncertainty captures embedding quality. This separation produces uncertainties that better reflect actual reliability.

\paragraph{SNGP comparison.}
SNGP~\citep{liu2020simple} estimates uncertainty via distance to training data in feature space. On KGs, this approach faces two limitations: (1) it remains relation-agnostic---an entity close to training data may still be OOD for a specific relation, and (2) random corruptions in KGs are far-OOD (completely implausible triples), which distance-based methods struggle to detect compared to score-based methods. SNGP achieves 0.614 AUROC on temporal OOD (Table~\ref{tab:icews})---better than ensembles but below structural uncertainty alone (0.824). On standard OOD (Table~\ref{tab:standard}), SNGP underperforms score-based methods because random corruptions are easily detected by low model scores. Our decomposition outperforms SNGP on both settings by explicitly modeling relation-specific structure.
