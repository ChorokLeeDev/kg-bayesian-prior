Every major probabilistic knowledge graph (KG) uncertainty method---variational embeddings, ensembles, energy scoring---shares a systematic blind spot: when familiar entities appear in novel relational contexts, these methods report low uncertainty despite having no training evidence. We show this blind spot affects 11--25\% of test queries across five benchmarks and causes existing methods to achieve near-random AUROC (0.38--0.59 on ICEWS14 novel contexts) on temporal OOD detection.

We prove an impossibility theorem explaining this failure: any uncertainty derived from standard KG embeddings achieves near-random detection on novel contexts, because embeddings encode semantic similarity but not which relations each entity has seen. This covers variance-based, ensemble, dropout, and energy methods. The result motivates decomposing KG uncertainty into \emph{semantic} (entity variance) and \emph{structural} (entity-relation coverage) components with predictable, non-overlapping failure modes.

On temporal KGs with ground-truth timestamps (ICEWS14/18/GDELT), a simple coverage check achieves 0.99--1.00 AUROC while all baselines collapse to chance under strict evaluation. Crucially, on GDELT with high coverage overlap ($\rho = 0.84$), semantic still achieves random AUROC (0.50)---confirming the impossibility holds regardless of $\rho$. On static benchmarks where emerging entities have partial coverage, semantic adds +8--11pp. The practical fix is trivial: track entity-relation coverage via hash table ($O(1)$ lookup, ${<}2\%$ overhead). The contribution is the diagnosis---identifying and explaining a pervasive failure mode that standard evaluation protocols have masked.
